When I moved from Lead PM to Director at Seven.One, HR updated my title in a day. Changing how I spent my week took the better part of a year.

As a senior or lead PM, you earn trust by knowing your product cold: the schema quirks, the last five A/B test results, the edge cases in checkout, and the exact ticket blocking the release. Once you manage multiple product lines and PMs, holding onto that habit actively hurts the team. If you are still the best-informed person in the room on every detail of every product, your PMs don't have enough room to lead.

The job shifts from **having the right answer** to **making sure the team can reach good answers without you in the room**. That requires clear priorities, explicit tradeoffs when two teams want the same engineering capacity, and enough business context so a PM can make a hard call on a Tuesday afternoon without waiting for a steering meeting.

In practice, my week changed in three ways:

- **Fewer PRDs, more framing documents.** I stopped red-lining individual feature specs and started spending that time on the quarterly strategy docs and budget cases that shape five or six specs at once.
- **Fewer stand-ups, more skip-levels and cross-team unblocking.** I dropped out of daily squad stand-ups unless a team asked me to join. Instead, I use that time for 1:1s with engineers, analysts, and commercial leads to spot organizational friction before it slows down a quarter.
- **A six-month horizon instead of a two-week sprint.** If the PMs are focused on what ships this month, my job is to figure out which vendor contracts, platform bottlenecks, or hiring gaps will bite us two quarters from now.

You don't stop caring about product quality when you become a Director. You just have to judge yourself by the quality of the decisions your team makes when you aren't on the call.
